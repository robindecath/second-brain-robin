# Method selection

Use this when the method choice is not obvious. Pick the method from the research question, not from habit or available tooling.

## Step 1 — Classify the question

| Question shape | Evidence needed | Family |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Why do people do this? What is the context? | Explanation | Qualitative |
| How many, how often, how much, how does A compare to B? | Measurement | Quantitative |
| Both explanation and magnitude | Both | Mixed |
| What problems exist at all? | Discovery | Exploratory |
| Does this specific design work? | Assessment | Evaluative |

## Step 2 — Match method to question

| Research question | Recommended method | Gives you | Does not give you |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| What unmet needs and workarounds exist? | Semi-structured interviews (4–8 per distinct group) | Motivations, context, mental models | Prevalence |
| How do people actually behave in context? | Contextual inquiry / field observation / diary study | Real environment, constraints, over-time behavior | Statistical generalization |
| Can users complete key tasks? Where do they fail? | Moderated usability test (5–8 per group) | Failure causes, friction, task success | Precise conversion impact |
| Which of two designs performs better at scale? | A/B test or unmoderated benchmark | Comparative performance | Reasons for the difference |
| How widespread is an issue we observed? | Survey with neutral closed questions | Prevalence, segment differences | Depth, causality |
| How do users group and label concepts? | Card sort / tree test | Information architecture fit | Visual design quality |
| Where does the end-to-end experience break? | Journey mapping workshop + supporting data | Cross-touchpoint view | Component-level usability |
| What is already known? | Analytics review, support-ticket mining, prior research audit | Cheap baseline evidence | New unmet needs |

Always start step 2 by checking existing evidence. Do not run a study that existing analytics, support tickets, or prior research already answers.

## Step 3 — Size the sample

- Qualitative interviews or usability tests: 4–8 per meaningfully distinct group. Add participants when the audience is diverse, risk is high, or new themes still appear. Stop when new sessions stop changing the picture.
- Surveys: size for the smallest segment you must report on, not for the total. State the margin of error.
- Unmoderated benchmarks: aim for enough completed sessions per condition to detect the effect size the team cares about; if that is not feasible, report it as directional.
- Never present a qualitative sample as a measurement of the population.

## Step 4 — State the tradeoffs

For every recommendation, name explicitly:

- What the method will answer well
- What it cannot answer
- The main threat to validity (sampling, hierarchy, novelty, ordering, moderator influence)
- The cheaper alternative you rejected and why
- The follow-up method that would raise confidence

## Triangulation patterns

- Analytics show *what* dropped; interviews explain *why*; a usability test confirms the mechanism.
- Interviews surface a theme; a survey estimates how widespread it is.
- A survey shows a low score; a diary study reveals the lived cause.

When qualitative and quantitative evidence disagree, report the disagreement rather than choosing the convenient side.
